About Fact or Fake

What is Fact or Fake?

What is it?

Fact or Fake is a research-based educational website that helps teenagers recognise deceptive links, fake websites and online impersonation scams. Visitors learn how these scams work, explore practical ways to check suspicious requests, and practise with a video and a 10-question quiz.

Who is it for?

Our website is designed for teenagers who use social media, shop online, and receive messages through apps, texts or email. We use clear explanations and relatable examples so the research connects to situations you may actually face.

What problem does it address?

Online content can look trustworthy without being genuine. A familiar logo, an attractive offer, or a message that seems to come from a friend may push someone to click, pay or share personal information.

Why does Fact or Fake matter?

We go beyond telling teenagers to "be careful online." Each explanation shows why something may be suspicious and how to investigate it, so visitors build habits like questioning unexpected requests and checking sources independently.

Sites: 2-column layout · text left, image right

Why use Fact or Fake alongside security tools?

Your device has security. Does your brain?

A firewall provides technical protection by controlling network traffic. Fact or Fake adds the human side, teaching visitors how to assess a suspicious message, offer or account before deciding what to do.

It's not just texts

Phishing has many disguises

Emails

The most common form of phishing [4]. Attackers often pose as big account providers or even a coworker.

Phone calls

Scammers can phish by voice too [1], [4]. They pose as a trusted organisation to get your details.

QR codes

One scan can open a fake website [1]. You never even see the link before it loads.

Fake support DMs

"Customer service" profiles on social media that send malicious links [1]. They look helpful, but they're the trap.

The damage

What one click can cost you

Your money

Phishing can lead to stolen money and fraudulent charges on your cards [4]. Almost 1 in 10 UK 8–17 year-olds have already lost money to an online scam [3].

Your accounts

Once scammers have your login, you can lose access to your own photos, videos and files [4]. Your private messages and memories end up in a stranger's hands.

Your identity

Some fake websites are built specifically for identity theft [1]. Visiting one can even download malware that keeps collecting your data in the background [1].

Your friends

A hacked account becomes the scammer's new disguise. Criminals can pretend to be you and put the people you know at risk [4], and your friends will trust "your" link just like you'd trust theirs [2].

The fix

Four moves that beat the scammer

Sites: 4-column card layout · section background = light
1

Hit pause

Scams are designed to rush you, so slowing down is your first defence. 86% of UK 8–17 year-olds already see pressure to act fast as a red flag [3]. Trust that instinct.

2

Inspect the clues

Check the sender's real email address, not just the display name, which can be faked [4]. Hover over or long-press a link to see where it actually goes [4].

3

Go around the message

Never use the link or number inside a suspicious message. Open the official app or type the website yourself, and confirm through a trusted, official channel [5].

4

Call in backup

Not sure? Check with a parent or trusted adult [2]. 72% of UK 8–17 year-olds would turn to a parent or carer if they thought they were being scammed [3].

Tab 3 · Fake Online Shops

Fake Online Shops: When the Deal Is the Trap

Real logos. Unreal prices. Sometimes nothing in the box.

The Bait The Damage The Fix
Sites: 2-column layout · text left, quote box right

Why this matters

You've probably already seen one today

Fake online shops and misleading social media ads can trick teenagers into trusting offers that are not real. They show up between posts from friends and creators, so they don't look out of place. The ad looks polished, the brand looks familiar, and the price looks like luck.

The problem is that a scam doesn't need to look like a scam to work. It only needs you to click "Shop now" before you check who you're really buying from.

"Unusually low prices are a sign of a scam."

The US Federal Trade Commission's shopping advice [6]

The bait

How fake shops win your trust

Sites: 2 × 2 text boxes

Borrowed brands

Scammers use pictures and logos from real brands to make fake shopping websites look trustworthy [6]. A fake site can look just as polished and professional as the real one. Recognising a brand doesn't mean you're on that brand's website.

Prices that pull you in

Social media ads can show expensive items like designer bags and scooters at very low prices [6]. The FTC warns that unusually low prices are a sign of a scam [6]. The bigger the discount, the more careful you should be.

Ads from impostors

Scammers impersonate real companies on social media and run ads offering big discounts on brand-name products [10]. The ad leads to a fake website designed to steal money or information [10]. The seller in the ad may not be the brand at all.

"Congratulations, you won!"

Fake giveaways are another way scammers trick people online. Scammers pretend to be a real business and tell people they have won prizes before asking for personal or financial information [8]. Because the message seems to come from a familiar company, it's easy to believe.

Why do teenagers fall for it?

Teenagers might trust these offers because they recognise the brand or think they're getting a good deal, without realising they could lose money. A familiar logo makes the shop feel safe, and an exciting deal makes you want to act before it disappears. Scammers rely on both feelings at once.

Sites: 4-column layout of big-number text boxes

By the numbers

Social media is where the shopping scams live

44%

of social media fraud-loss reports in the first half of 2023 were about online shopping [7]. It was the most reported type of social media scam.

61%

of those online shopping reports were about orders that never arrived [7]. Clothing and electronics were the items people mentioned most.

1 in 4

people who reported losing money to fraud since 2021 said it started on social media [7]. Reported losses added up to $2.7 billion [7].

47%

of 18–19 year-olds who reported losing money to fraud said it started on social media [7]. That was the highest share of any age group.

All figures are reports to the US Federal Trade Commission, published October 2023 [7]. They describe social media fraud in general unless stated, not only fake shops.

The damage

What a fake shop can take from you

Sites: 2 × 2 text boxes · section background = darkest

Nothing in the box

Another problem with fake online shops is that customers might not receive what they paid for. People who buy from fake shopping websites might receive a different product than they ordered, or nothing at all [6]. If something does arrive, it may be a cheap knockoff [10].

Your payment details

Teenagers could lose their money after trusting a website that looks real, even if the products and ads seem genuine. They might also put their personal or payment information at risk when entering their details. Fake shopping sites are built to steal money or information such as bank account numbers [10].

Your identity

If you share personal information with a fake seller, the scammer may use it to steal your identity [10]. A "great deal" can end up costing far more than its price tag.

Your device

Links in fake giveaway messages might contain malware [8]. One tap on a "claim your prize" link can put your phone, and everything on it, at risk.

Glow StudioDirect message
Congratulations! You've been picked as the winner of our giveaway. Just enter your card details to claim your prize. glowstudio-prize-claim.net

Glow Studio is a made-up brand.

How fake giveaways work

Why would a free prize need your card number?

Scammers watch the followers of real businesses, then hijack real giveaways or pretend to be the business [8]. After you like, tag and share, someone who looks like the business owner tags you as a winner and asks for financial information to claim your prize [8].

Red alert All clear

Shop smarter.

Check the seller before you check out.

The fix

Six checks before you buy or claim

Teenagers can reduce the risk of fake online shops by checking whether a seller is trustworthy before buying anything. None of these checks takes more than a minute.

Sites: 3-column card layout · section background = light
1

Check the seller

Look at seller ratings, recent reviews, contact information and return policies before you buy [9]. A real shop makes it easy to contact them and return things.

2

Don't trust reviews alone

Search for complaints about the seller and compare reviews from different websites [9]. Some reviews can be fake or misleading, so a page full of five stars isn't proof.

3

Search the name

Search the seller's name with words like "review", "complaint" or "scam" to see what other buyers say [6], [10]. Don't assume the seller is the brand named in the ad [10].

4

Compare the price

Research the seller and compare prices with other shops, especially when products are advertised at unusually low prices [10]. If it's much, much cheaper than everywhere else, it's probably a scam [10].

5

Watch how they want paying

Never buy from a seller who insists on gift cards, wire transfers, payment apps or cryptocurrency [6], [10]. The FTC says only scammers demand payment that way [10].

6

Confirm giveaways at the source

Contact the business through its official website or contact details to confirm whether a giveaway is real [8]. Avoid clicking links in the prize message itself.

Tab 4 · Impersonation and AI

Impersonation and AI: When the Voice Isn't Really Theirs

It sounds like family. It looks like a friend. It might be neither.

The Trick The Damage The Fix
Sites: 2-column layout · text left, quote box right

Why this matters

Your ears and eyes can be fooled

Impersonation scams used to depend on a convincing story. Now, with artificial intelligence, scammers can copy the voice or face of someone you know. That makes it difficult to recognise a scam when you hear a familiar voice or see a familiar face.

This tab explains how these scams work, why they're so convincing, and the simple steps that stop them, even when the voice sounds exactly right.

Scammers can clone a family member's voice to make a fake emergency seem real.

The US Federal Trade Commission [11]

The trick

How impersonators fool you

Sites: 2 × 2 text boxes

Someone you trust

Impersonation is a type of scam in which criminals pretend to be someone trustworthy to steal money or personal information. For example, scammers might pretend to be a friend, a family member or a company employee. The more familiar they seem, the less likely you are to question them.

Cloned voices

With AI, these scams have become more convincing because scammers can use cloned voices to imitate real people [11]. AI-generated voices copy how a person sounds [11], [12]. A call can sound exactly like someone you know without being them.

Deepfake videos

Deepfake videos can make someone appear to say or do things they never actually did [11], [12]. A video call or clip of a familiar face is no longer proof of who you're talking to.

The fake emergency

A scammer calls pretending to be a relative in trouble, then asks you to send money right away [11]. The urgency is designed to stop you from checking the story first [11].

Why does it work so well?

People are more likely to act when a call sounds like their boss or a family member [11]. Add a panicked voice and a deadline, and most people want to help first and ask questions later. Scammers count on that kindness.

Sites: 3-column layout of text boxes

Spot the script

Three lines impersonators love to use

"I'm in trouble"

The caller sounds like a relative and says something has gone wrong [11]. The emotional shock is meant to make you react, not think.

"Send it right now"

The request for money comes with a deadline [11]. Real emergencies can wait the two minutes it takes to call back on a number you know.

"It's your boss"

Some scammers clone the voice of someone in charge and ask for things like bank account numbers [11]. Authority makes people less likely to question the request.

Sites: 4-column layout of big-number text boxes

By the numbers

Impersonation is big business for scammers

$3.5B

People reported losing $3.5 billion to impersonation scams in 2025 [12]. This includes impersonation in general, not only AI-related scams.

~3×

Reported losses to impersonation scams have risen nearly three times since 2020 [12]. The problem is growing fast.

1 in 3

Nearly one in three fraud reports in 2025 were about impersonation scams [12]. That made it the most reported type of fraud.

$1B

Nearly $1 billion was reported lost to scammers pretending to be businesses [12]. Bank impersonators caused the highest reported losses.

All figures are reports to the US Federal Trade Commission for 2025 [12]. They cover impersonation scams in general, which highlights the financial harm these crimes can cause.

The damage

What one convincing call can cost

Sites: 2 × 2 text boxes · section background = darkest

Your money

Victims of impersonation scams may lose money [13]. A single panicked transfer to a "relative" in trouble can be all it takes.

Your personal information

Impersonators can steal your personal information [13]. Details you share with a "familiar" voice can be used against you later.

Your accounts

Victims may experience unauthorised access to their accounts [13]. Once someone is in, your messages and private details are no longer private.

Your reputation

Fake videos can spread misinformation or damage someone's reputation [13]. A deepfake can make it look like a person said or did something they never did [11], [12].

Red alert All clear

Verify, don't trust.

A realistic voice or video is not proof.

The fix

Four steps when a familiar voice asks for money

Teenagers can protect themselves from impersonation scams by verifying unexpected requests before sending money or sharing personal information, even when the voice, video or account seems familiar.

Sites: 4-column card layout · section background = light
1

Don't respond straight away

Be careful with urgent requests through calls, messages or video chats. Scammers may pretend to be in an emergency to pressure people into sending money quickly [11].

2

Call back on a known number

Contact the person directly using a phone number you already know, instead of trusting the voice alone [11]. If the emergency is real, they'll confirm it.

3

Can't reach them? Ask family

If you can't get hold of the person, try other family members or their friends [11]. Check the story before you send anything.

4

Ask a trusted adult

If you're unsure, ask a trusted adult for help before you act. These steps can reduce the risk of losing money or exposing private information.

?

1

Stop. Don't send anything yet.

The pressure is part of the trick [11].

2

Use a number you already know, not one the caller gives you [11].

How we checked our facts

Three rules we followed

Every number is labelled

Each statistic shows its year, country and who it describes. UK figures come from a survey of 8–17 year-olds; US figures come from reports to the Federal Trade Commission.

General vs. specific

We separate data about scams in general from data about one type of scam. For example, the $3.5 billion figure covers all impersonation scams, not only AI ones.

Made-up examples

All brands and names in our examples (Pixora, StepUp Kicks, Glow Studio, Cousin Sara) are invented, so no real company or person is copied.
